## 1. The recommendation in one sentence

**Charter the India site as the accountable owner of whole platform components — telemetry ingestion and the experimentation engine first, data-quality and contracts tooling next — and of one domain, quality of experience, with sessionization and the metrics catalog staying central; graduate a second domain (fraud and paid sharing, or ads measurement) on a criteria-gated decision; and keep commerce, experimentation analysis, browse, search and conversion funnels anchored in the US.**

It is a hybrid that transfers ownership by whole units on written gates. After Gate 0 there is no phase in which India contributes to work the US owns; before Gate 0 — up to six months — the contractors continue to do exactly that, and the memo does not pretend otherwise. It *does* graduate: the honest distinction from the "graduating extension" it rejects is the starting state at the first transfer — whole ownership, including on-call — not the presence of gates. **[judgment]**

The memo also says what it is betting on. In Phase 1 the site is an execution site with local management, operating two components under US-authored standards; what India decides unilaterally in Phase 1 is listed in §3, and it is thin. The bet is that Phase 2 changes that — and Phase 2 has a test whose failure is named and costed.

### Why this and not the alternatives — the defense

**The evidence rules out the two pure options and the pure extension.**

- *Full domain ownership of commerce or funnels* has no documented success case for a new centre. The India organizations that owned a consumer domain end-to-end — Hotstar, Google Pay — were India-first products with a local P&L [C5-33][C5-35] `[documented]`. Commerce has the most US decision-makers per change (pricing, bundling, finance, legal, trust & safety) and each of those decisions is a cross-site work item; Herbsleb and Mockus measured such items at about 2.5x the calendar time of single-site items, driven by more people per item [C3-43][C5-18] `[documented]`. Hotstar is *not* evidence that domain charters fail: its dispersal followed the rights economics and shows that a self-contained site follows its market's P&L, which is true of any charter [C2-16] `[inferred, single trade source]`.
- *Pure platform ownership* is the modal centre charter and it scales — JPMorgan at ~55,000, WBD's India hub, Lowe's platform VPs [C5-27][C5-31][C5-25] `[documented]`. The claim that it *drifts* is a structural argument, not a documented base rate: the divestitures are 2008–09 BPO/IT-support captives [C5-13], the "captives become stagnant" line is one practitioner [C5-15], and Sky's 2025 technology cuts are a consolidation [C2-38]. **[judgment]** The memo adds one domain as a hedge against a plausible drift, and §5B says so.
- *A graduating extension* — India contributes to US-owned work until criteria are met — is the configuration in which ownership is diffuse by design, and diffuse ownership is the best-replicated defect predictor in the software-engineering literature: Microsoft's organizational metrics predicted failure-prone components at ~86% precision [C3-8] `[documented]`. The market picture is a snapshot, not a rate: Zinnov's ladder puts 56% of India centres in its two execution tiers and 44% in its two ownership tiers; Zinnov separately claims 27% reach its portfolio tier within five years, by a method it does not publish [C5-1] `[documented, consultant]`. The two celebrated graduations, Target and Lowe's, took roughly two decades and ten years and had product management, data science and managers in India by the time ownership was described as theirs — the sequence is not documented [C5-22][C5-24][C5-25] `[documented, single interview and company pages]`. Nothing in a US owner's incentives pushes a hand-off later [C3-1] **[judgment]**.

**The evidence supports one shape.** Of the remote sites in the case studies, the five chartered as new hubs by a parent — Netflix Warsaw, Spotify London, Amazon Hyderabad, Prime Video Bengaluru, WBD India — hold capability charters [C1-6][C1-21][C1-35][C1-36][C2-22] `[documented; WBD's is careers-page copy]`. Google Zurich grew into product-surface ownership over about eighteen years; Sky/Osterley is the *origin* site of the Peacock platform; Spotify Boston kept the charter it was acquired with [C1-27][C2-28][C1-22] `[documented]`. None is a data organization; the data-org literature does not treat geography at all [C3 §8]. The distributed-work research says distance is survivable for separable work with clear ownership and shared tooling — Microsoft found no measurable quality penalty for distributed components once ownership was site-level [C3-9] — and costly for coupled work with diffuse ownership [C3-43][C3-45][C5-19] `[documented]`. Target's operating rule twenty-one years in — "ownership of outcomes resting with where the center of gravity for the capability sits" — is one executive's sentence in one interview, and the best description of a working hybrid the research found [C5-22] `[documented, single source]`.

**Why one domain, and which one — scored against the decision rights it would actually have.** A platform-only site has no customer on its own site and nothing the US cannot ship without. The research tracks did not converge on a first domain: C3 argued for defence-heavy fraud and commerce data quality; C5 named QoE and sessions *telemetry* as a capability; C1 and C2 named QoE or sessions as candidates for *graduating* domain ownership, with C2 listing fraud first **[judgment]**. The choice is this memo's, made against three tests derived from that research — few US decision-makers per change; standardizable outputs; separability from the warehouse's reciprocal dependencies — plus a legal gate (cross-border personal data) and, named honestly because it decides the Phase 3 choice, a fifth: robustness to a P&L-driven re-homing of the data org, which the legacy-media cases say happens every two to three years [C2-11][C2-43]. Each candidate is scored under the decision-rights table it would be given, not under a US-approves-everything arrangement the memo bans:

| Candidate | US decision-makers per change (under India-decides, US-consulted rights) | Outputs standardize | Separable from warehouse | Cross-border data | Outcome the parent notices | Robust to re-homing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

QoE wins on separability and legal exposure and is the smallest outcome and the least robust to re-homing. The fifth criterion does not bind the first-domain choice because its downside is the costed Gate-2-failure landing — a platform site — whereas legal exposure has no such landing; it binds Phase 3 because by then the site can afford to optimize for survival. Fraud wins on relevance and loses on legal exposure and the dollar latency of band changes. **QoE is the first domain — with a month-3 re-decision point (§2, Gate 0): if privacy counsel clears fraud as scoped and the contractor concentration favours commerce, Phase 2 becomes fraud and QoE becomes a capability.** Fraud or ads measurement is the month-18 candidate otherwise. **Sessionization stays central.** If QoE fails its Phase 2 test, the domain claim is withdrawn at the next annual review, the QoE metrics roadmap continues as a platform product owned by the same India lead, and the site is a platform site; that outcome is costed in §3 and it is a failure of the memo's bet, not "acceptable." **[judgment]**

**Why "whole components" rather than "the platform," and where the experimentation line is drawn.** Moving the whole platform on day one would strip the US of platform architecture while the site has no senior leadership — the vacuum Zinnov documents [C5-9]. Team Topologies allows a platform to be several teams with clean interfaces [C3-13]; Amazon's correction to the two-pizza rule was that dependencies, not size, predict failure [C1-31] `[documented]`. So components move one at a time, each with one owner on one site. The experimentation engine is the hard case because its main customers — analysis and the review gate — stay in the US. v2 drew the line at "India computes, the US defines"; walked through three quarterly changes (a new guardrail metric needing a schema extension; a redefinition of active user forcing in-flight recomputation; a sample-ratio-mismatch bug found in the US morning) it produced four hand-offs per change and a provider that could not say no. **The line in v3: India owns the runtime — assignment, exposure logging, metrics computation — and the metric-definition schema, with a published change process and service levels; the US analysis function is a customer that files definition requests and owns the semantic catalog entry and the review gate.** A new metric expressible in the schema is a request served within a stated SLA; a definition request within the schema carries a stated SLA, and a schema extension carries a committed-quarter answer — T4 measures both; a definition change that forces recomputation splits cleanly: India decides the scheduling and mechanics of recomputation under the SLA, and the review gate decides validity — which experiments' readouts are accepted under which definition — a decision India's schedule must honour, counted as the one hand-off; an SRM-detected exposure bug is a P1 that pages India's on-call at whatever hour, because that is what owning the component means, and the gate measures P1 hours-to-resolve across sites, not P1 count. That is X-as-a-service in Team Topologies' sense — the provider evolves the service on its own roadmap and the consumer uses it with minimal collaboration [C3-14]. The engine has one customer on its own site: the QoE team supplies QoE guardrail metrics to experiments, which is standard practice in streaming experimentation. The three walk-throughs are on the Applying It page so the hand-off count is visible. **[judgment]**

**Why not fraud first (the strongest alternative).** Fraud is defence-heavy, output-standardized, stable in definition, and coordinated by standardized outputs [C3-4][C3-42]. Scored under the decision-rights table it would have, its US decision-makers per change are no denser than QoE's. It loses the first slot on one ground only: personal data may make it non-transferable as scoped, and that is unknowable until month 3. Hence the month-3 re-decision point rather than a month-18 wait: if counsel clears it and the contractors sit in commerce, fraud is Phase 2. If counsel clears it and the contractors sit in platform and QoE (the assumed baseline), QoE stays first because the transfer is a change of ownership rather than of hands, and fraud is the month-18 candidate. **[judgment]**

**If Gate 2 fails.** The domain claim is withdrawn at the next annual review. India keeps telemetry, the QoE pipelines and the QoE metrics roadmap as a platform product with the same lead; the definitions are registered in the central catalog; the US liaison seat closes. Cost of the failed bet: the QoE analytics lead's role narrows (a retention risk, budgeted at one senior backfill), and the US QoE team's dissolution stands — the org does not re-form it. The site is a platform site with local management. That is the outcome §5B calls acceptable *as a site*; it is a failure *of this memo's bet*, and the annual review says so. Gate 3 is then judged without the Gate 2 criterion, against the platform charter, with "the platform-product roadmap in the annual plan" in its place. **[judgment]**

Thresholds are "baseline plus its own variance," and cohort measures are in heads, not percentages, for cohorts under 30.

## 3. Implications for the whole 140-person org

**What India owns first, and why.** Telemetry ingestion and the experimentation engine go first because they have the cleanest interfaces (a landing schema; a metric-definition schema with a change process), few US decision-makers per change, and — on the assumed baseline — are where contractors already work. The line between the two India teams that touch playback events: **ingestion owns raw events to the contracted landing schema; QoE owns everything downstream of it.** DQ tooling goes with QoE because its first customers are the India ingestion and QoE teams — it has a customer on its own site from day one.

**What India decides unilaterally in Phase 1** (the honest list): pipeline internals and deployment; on-call response and remediation; the landing-schema *implementation* and its versioning; the metric-definition schema and its change process and SLAs; the experimentation runtime's roadmap. **What it does not:** warehouse modeling conventions, governance policy, semantic metric definitions, architecture standards (the US publishes them and audits against them). In Phase 1 the site is an execution site with local management under US-authored standards. Phase 2 is where India starts deciding definitions.

**What the US org stops doing.** This is the part that fails in practice, so it is explicit:
- US platform engineers stop owning, gatekeeping and carrying on-call for the transferred components. They keep sessionization and the semantic layer, warehouse modeling, governance and access, the ML platform (until Phase 3), the semantic catalog entries, and platform architecture as a shared standard, not a veto. The US engineers who ran the existing freshness and observability checks are in the Phase 2(c) transfer: their seats are in the offset scenario below.
- The US QoE team is dissolved at G1, announced at G1 − 3, with retention to G1 + 3 and a documented facilitating-mode period (G1 − 3 to G1 + 3) as the transfer mechanism.
- No US manager has India directs. Every India engineer reports to an India manager; every India manager reports to the site head. During Phase 1 the site head runs the teams through tech leads until the managers start (stated in §2).
- Half-ownership is banned as a category after G0, with two stated, time-boxed exceptions: the QoE facilitating-mode window, and the T3 quarter-4 shared state.

**Reporting lines.** The site head is a peer of the US directors on the data executive's staff, with a global functional title and accountability for the transferred components and domains — not a country manager [C5-3][C5-9]. The whole org stays one hub-and-spoke: central reporting line and hiring bar, people aligned to verticals, the org (not the business partner) prioritizing scarce talent — the Netflix shape as of its 2020–2022 posts, at an order of magnitude smaller [C1-1]. Experimentation analysis and the catalog report centrally in the US. The QoE decision-rights table gives India the decision and the US engineering consumers a consultation right; NBCUniversal's "embedded with and equally accountable to" [C2-33] is cited as a case, not adopted as the template — equal accountability is the diffuse ownership this memo bans.

**Headcount and cost (assumption-dependent; finance replaces every number).** Conversion is a per-head cost step-up, derived from the vendor rate: a $60k bill rate at a 30–40% vendor margin pays the engineer $36–42k; the 25–40% centre premium [C5-11] puts the base at $45–59k; loaded at 1.3× that is $58–77k. Against a boutique-heavy contractor mix billing $80–100k a seat, conversion is a per-head saving — assuming boutique premiums sit in vendor margin and onshore overhead rather than engineer pay; if the released roster shows otherwise, the conversion cost and the acceptance gate both move. ## 5. Steelmanned alternatives

### A. Full domain ownership — give India commerce end-to-end
**Strongest case.** Hotstar proves an India engineering org can own a streaming product at record concurrency and export its platform [C5-33]. Amazon's lesson is to move the decision-maker with the team [C1-31]. Contractors already touch commerce pipelines; commerce has the clearest P&L. Decisive ownership beats a decade-long graduation.
**Why it still loses.** Hotstar and Google Pay were India-first products with local P&L; there is no verified case of a *global* consumer domain handed to a new centre on day one succeeding [C5 §5]. Commerce packaging has the densest US stakeholder set; every pricing and bundling decision is a cross-site work item at ~2.5x [C3-43]. On the assumed baseline the site has no site head, no product owner and no engineering managers. The commerce sub-domain with the best case, fraud, is scored in §1 and has a month-3 re-decision point.

### B. Pure platform/capability ownership — India is the data platform, nothing else
**Strongest case.** The modal successful centre charter [C5-26][C2-22][C5-25]; platform work is coordinated by standardization, which distance tolerates [C3-4][C3-9]; interfaces are APIs and schemas; it is what the contractors already do; fastest to charter and easiest to defend to a CFO. The recommendation is eighty percent this option, and if Gate 2 fails it *is* this option.
**Why the memo adds one domain anyway.** Not because drift is documented for technology-platform sites — it is not [C5-13][C5-15]. Because a platform with no customer on its own site loses the platform-as-product feedback loop [C3-15], builds a junior-heavy pyramid with no path to the managers the site will need [C5-6], and gives a future P&L-driven owner nothing to notice. One domain is a hedge with a test that costs something to pass (decommissioning) and something to fail (a withdrawn claim and a senior backfill). **[judgment]**

### C. Graduating extension — start as an extension of US teams, graduate on criteria
**Strongest case.** Lowest immediate risk; it lets the org learn who the converted people are before committing ownership; Target and Lowe's are real graduations [C5-22][C5-24]; Olson & Olson say common ground must precede remote collaboration, and an extension phase builds it [C3-44]; Galbraith's lateral-relations-first sequencing is respectable theory [C3-2].
**Why it still loses.** During the extension phase ownership is diffuse — the Nagappan defect predictor and Spotify's "responsibility without accountability" [C3-8][C1-13]; nothing in the incentive structure pushes a US owner to hand off; and the celebrated graduations took ten to twenty years. The recommendation keeps the mechanism and changes the starting state at the first transfer: whole ownership including on-call. It concedes that Phase 0 is an extension and that Phase 1 is execution under US standards; the difference is that the transfer date, the decision rights and the test are written before Phase 0 starts.

### D. Undifferentiated hybrid — decide per team, case by case
**Strongest case.** Target's centre-of-gravity rule is a hybrid and it worked [C5-22]; Walmart runs global platforms next to a separately owned local product company [C5-28][C5-30]; domains differ [C3-42].
**Why it still loses.** "Hybrid" is what mature sites became [C5 §5]; without naming which units on day one, per-team choice produces half-ownership because each US owner chooses "extension" for their own team [C3-8][C1-31]. The recommendation is a hybrid with the choices made, scored, and re-decidable at month 3.

## 7. What remains uncertain

- The headcount baseline and the contractor mix are assumed; §6 shows where the plan is sensitive.
- Everything about decision-rights frameworks, budget ownership and on-call at India centres is consultant material [C5 §3]; the decision-rights tables here are judgment.
- Cross-border personal data is a Phase 0 legal question this research could not answer; it may rule out fraud as scoped and it touches QoE telemetry too.
- No source treats domain-vs-capability ownership at a remote site for data teams specifically [C3 §8]; the memo extrapolates from software-engineering distance research and centre snapshots.
- The Zinnov distribution and the attrition figures come from firms with a commercial interest in centres looking good [C5-1][C5-7]. The cost table is illustrative; finance replaces it.
- Attribution is weak everywhere; this memo rests on avoiding documented failure modes more than on copying documented successes.
