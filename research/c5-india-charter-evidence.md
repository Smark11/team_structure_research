# C5 — India site charter: what the evidence actually discriminates

Private memo. Research agent C5, 2026-08-29. Scope: evidence that helps choose between (a) domain ownership, (b) platform/capability ownership, (c) graduating extension, (d) hybrid for a ~140-person data org converting India contractors to FTEs.

Reading guide: `[documented]` = verified primary or credible secondary source; `[inferred]` = my reading of documented facts; `[folklore]` = widely repeated, not verifiable. Numbers from Zinnov/EY/ANSR are consultant surveys with a commercial interest in GCCs looking good; I say so where it matters.

---

## 1. Which GCC charters succeeded vs. stagnated

**The market picture first.** India has 2,117 GCCs across 3,728 units employing ~2.36M people, $98.4B revenue (FY26) [C5-1] `[documented]`, up from ~1,700 GCCs / 1.9M / $64.6B in FY24 [C5-2]. Roughly 110–120 new GCCs are set up per year [C5-3][C5-5]. Zinnov's maturity ladder (Outpost → Satellite → Portfolio Hub → Transformation Hub) puts the current population at 13% / 43% / 39% / 5% [C5-1]. Translation: **56% of India GCCs are still execution shops** (Outpost/Satellite), and only about 27% reach "Portfolio Hub" (real end-to-end ownership of a portfolio) within five years [C5-1] `[documented, consultant-sourced]`. That 27%-in-five-years number is the single best base rate available for "an extension that graduates," and it comes from the firm selling GCC set-ups, so treat it as an upper bound `[inferred]`.

**Target in India (Bengaluru, 2005→).** Started in 2005 via ANSR "with a small team focused on building IT capabilities to support the US headquarters" [C5-23] `[documented]`. Now 5,700+ people, ~40% technology, described by its president Andrea Zimmerman as an "integrated global headquarters" with co-located Business, Tech, Product, Data and UX teams; India's store-design team does about half of new store designs and 80% of remodels; its fulfillment-science team scores ~2,000 nodes for order sourcing [C5-22] `[documented, single interview source]`. The operating principle she states is the useful part: **"ownership of outcomes rests with where the center of gravity for the capability sits."** [C5-22] That is not "India owns domain X"; it is "whichever site has the critical mass of a capability owns its outcomes" — a hybrid by design, reached after ~15 years. ANSR's claim that Bengaluru is "25% of Target's global workforce" is nonsense against Target's ~400k headcount; it can only mean HQ/corporate workforce [C5-23] `[inferred]`. Note what Target did NOT do: it did not carve out a customer-facing domain and hand it to India on day one. It grew capabilities (data science, product, UX) alongside engineering until the center of gravity moved.

**Walmart Global Tech India (Bengaluru, Chennai, Gurugram).** Walmart's own description is that India teams "build technology solutions to help millions of Walmart customers around the world," across e-commerce, supply chain and payments [C5-28] `[documented]`; Chennai was entered in 2021 [C5-29]. Global WGT headcount was 20,000+ in 2022 [C5-29]. India headcount is not company-disclosed; aggregators put it at ~11k–15k and the 2024–26 lease footprint (9.5 lakh sq ft Bengaluru, 4.65 lakh Chennai) is consistent with a low-to-mid five-figure site `[inferred]`. Flipkart (Walmart ~85%) and PhonePe are separate operating companies, not GCC charters [C5-30] `[documented]`. Claims in aggregator profiles that Bengaluru "owns" global search, pricing and payments could not be verified against a Walmart statement `[folklore until verified]`. What is documented is a large site with global-platform work and a separately-owned local product company sitting next to it — a structural hybrid.

**Lowe's India (Bengaluru, 2015→).** Opened Feb 2015 as a "Global Innovation Center" of ~500 focused on customer experience, technology and analytics; the MD's stated model was "one team, multiple locations" [C5-24] `[documented]` — i.e., an explicit extension charter. Ten years later it is 5,000+ associates across technology, analytics, business operations, finance, product management and shared services, with India-based VPs owning Applied AI/Data & Analytics, Omnichannel Platforms and Engineering Platforms, under an SVP who is both CTO and MD [C5-25] `[documented]`. That is a graduating-extension case that ended in platform-and-capability ownership with India-resident product management — not customer-domain P&L ownership.

**JPMorgan (Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad + Noida, GIFT City).** 50,000+ in Aug 2023 [C5-26], ~55,000 in 2026, adding ~1,000 technologists in cloud, cybersecurity and "AI data pipelines"; a 2M sq ft Mumbai campus for ~30,000 by 2029 [C5-27] `[documented]`. JPMorgan's own language is consistently "technology solutions, business and functional support to serve all of our clients globally" and "supports every division of the firm" [C5-26]. At 55k people this is the largest technology-and-operations *support* charter in the country — enormously successful as a cost and capacity engine, and still described by the parent as support, not ownership `[inferred from company language]`. Lesson: scale does not by itself produce a domain-ownership charter; banks have kept product and risk ownership near the regulator and the P&L.

**Media/streaming reference points.**
- *Hotstar* was built in India by Star India's Novi Digital from 2015, scaled to record concurrency (25.3M in 2019; ~59M in 2023), and its UX/platform was reused for Disney+ in Southeast Asia, Israel, South Africa and MENA [C5-33] `[documented]`. It is the strongest evidence that an India engineering org can own a streaming product end-to-end — but it was a *home-market product with local P&L*, not a GCC charter, and it is now majority-owned by Reliance (JioStar 63/37) [C5-33]. Reports of 2023 tech layoffs after Disney pulled tech into Burbank are widely repeated but I could not verify them `[folklore]`.
- *Warner Bros. Discovery* runs an "India Innovation Hub" across Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune, opened Hyderabad in Sept 2023 with ~1,200 planned in year one [C5-32], and describes it as "fundamental to building our global technology platforms, which power our suite of products worldwide" [C5-31] `[documented]`. Platform charter, explicitly.
- *Netflix* has no engineering site in India: its Mumbai office lists Content, Marketing, PR, Product and Operations only [C5-34] `[documented]`. The Blind explanation (senior-heavy culture incompatible with India's junior-heavy pyramid) is `[folklore]`.
- *Google* and *Amazon* both run very large India engineering organizations. Google's Bengaluru campus post says "we have been building from India, for the world," citing Google Pay and flood forecasting [C5-35] `[documented]`; Amazon's India office pages headline "Built for India, scaled globally" [C5-36] `[documented]`. Pattern: the products India owned first were **India-first products that later globalized**, not global products handed over.

**What distinguished owners from back offices** `[inferred across cases]`: (1) India teams first owned something whose customers or center of gravity were *local* (Hotstar, Google Pay, Target's store design for a US-store network was the exception, but it was a self-contained capability with clear outputs); (2) product management and data science were hired in India alongside engineering (Target, Lowe's) rather than left in the US; (3) a site head with a global functional title, not just a country title — two-thirds of "Mega GCC" heads have technical backgrounds and many run a global portfolio in addition to India [C5-3]; (4) time — the successes are 10–20-year stories. The stagnation cases (next section) are almost all "support" charters that never got a product or platform to own.

## 2. Contractor→FTE conversion: documented failure modes

The historical record is about *captives* generally, not contractor conversion specifically; the failure mechanisms transfer.

- **Base rate of trouble.** Forrester (Apte, 2007) found "more than 60% of the captive centers in India alone are struggling," citing lack of management support, spiraling costs, skyrocketing attrition and lack of integration; it also observed that many captives were started because an expatriate executive wanted to move home for personal reasons rather than for strategy [C5-12] `[documented]`. That is 2007 data; the 2023 population is healthier (Everest: 452 new set-ups in 2023; ~50 captives sold in 2020–23 out of 1,450 created) [C5-15] `[documented]`.
- **Divestiture wave 2008–09.** Citi sold Citigroup Global Services to TCS (Oct 2008, ~$505M) and its Indian IT unit to Wipro (2009), tied to $2.5B/9.5-year and $500M/6-year service contracts; AXA's 600-person captive went to Capita; AOL, Aviva, Prudential UK and Philips put captives on the block [C5-13][C5-17] `[documented]`. Analyst reasons at the time: large fixed costs, management distraction, inflexible capacity in downturns, and — critically — "Indian professionals prefer vendor employment for career growth" [C5-13]. That last point has inverted: GCCs now pay 15–40% above IT services at mid-senior levels and have the lowest attrition of any segment [C5-4][C5-11] `[documented, 2025–26]`. The vendor-to-captive talent perception problem is a 2009 problem, not a 2026 one `[inferred]`.
- **Knowledge loss in the transfer.** Forrester's Bill Martorelli on build-operate-transfer: "Don't expect the service provider to be anxious to simply transfer all of their best people to you" [C5-15] `[documented]`. Forrester's Aase noted that companies that had offshored to vendors "did not retain valuable skills and knowledge" [C5-16]. Converting your current contractors is the same transaction: the vendor controls who is offered and who is counter-offered `[inferred]`.
- **Non-solicit / non-compete clauses.** I could not find a verified public source documenting specific vendor non-solicitation terms or a litigated case; this is standard MSA practice but goes here as `[folklore/unverified]`. Do not assume you can hire your own contractors without a negotiated release fee.
- **Salary-band shock.** GCC hikes average 9.8% with AI/ML specialists at 21.1% and niche skills commanding ~1.7x the standard hike [C5-7][C5-8] `[documented, Zinnov]`. Vendor bill rates hide individual pay; converting at market GCC bands means paying materially more per head than the vendor pays the same person — or accepting that only the people the market doesn't want will accept `[inferred]`.
- **Leadership vacuum.** Zinnov (2026): "the first three hiring decisions shape the center more than the next ninety-seven combined"; some centers run past 100 employees with no permanent head; hiring managers sequentially after ICs produces a site with no bridge to the global org [C5-9] `[documented, consultant]`. Momentum "can quickly sag as a result of executive turnover or loss of interest" (Martorelli) [C5-15].
- **The "same people, different badge" trap.** No single documented case, but the mechanism is documented in pieces: converting an execution team without adding PM, data science, and second-line managers leaves a Satellite-stage site (43% of the population) [C5-1]; West Monroe's Borowski: captives "become stagnant with marginal improvements over time" without external pressure [C5-15] `[documented]`.

## 3. Conditions under which India ownership works

**Leadership seeding.** Evidence favors a site head with a global functional mandate, hired first, over either a country-manager or a lift-and-shift of vendor delivery leads [C5-3][C5-9] `[documented]`. Forrester's 2007 data warns specifically against the returning-expat-for-personal-reasons pattern [C5-12]. EY's 2025 survey found ~80% of centers have under 10% of leadership roles based in India [C5-6] — the norm is still thin local leadership — while 52% report "shared accountability for global decisions" and 26% are "formally consulted" [C5-4]. Global roles led from India: 6,500+ in 2024, projected 30,000 by 2030 [C5-2][C5-3]. Zinnov's sharpest scarcity call is second-line managers "who can own outcomes... without constant escalation to the country head" [C5-6] `[documented, consultant]`. Nothing rigorous exists on "send a US leader vs. hire local"; the boomerang pattern is `[folklore]`.

**Time-zone interface design — the research that matters.**
- Herbsleb & Mockus (2003, IEEE TSE): distributed work items "take about two and one-half times as long to complete as similar items where all the work is colocated," and the mechanism is that "distributed work items involve more people," with headcount-per-item strongly predicting calendar time [C5-18] `[documented]`. The delay is not in the wire; it is in needing more people to resolve each change when expertise is split across sites.
- Cummings, Espinosa & Pickering (2009, ISR, 108 teams / 22 countries): time-zone separation hurts performance more than spatial separation, because temporal boundaries are harder to cross with tooling [C5-19] `[documented]`.
- Carmel & Agarwal (2001, IEEE Software): the tactics are to *reduce intensive collaboration* (modular, well-defined work division), reduce cultural distance, and reduce temporal distance [C5-20] `[documented]`.
- Ramasubbu & Balan (2007, ESEC/FSE): dispersion "significantly reduces development productivity" and hurts conformance quality; structured processes mitigate a large part of it [C5-21] `[documented]`.

Direct implication for a US-Pacific/India split (overlap ~0–2 hours): **any charter that requires shared ownership of the same work items across sites will run at roughly 2–2.5x calendar time**. The only charters the research supports are ones where India owns whole modules with their own decision rights, and the cross-site interface is an API/contract, not a stand-up `[inferred from C5-18–21]`.

**Decision rights, PM presence, on-call, budget.** The documented owners all have product management in India (Target: Business/Tech/Product/Data/UX co-located; Lowe's: product management as a listed function; Hotstar: whole company) [C5-22][C5-25][C5-33] `[documented]`. I found no documented data on RAPID/DACI adoption, on-call or budget ownership at India GCCs — that layer is consultant-decks-only `[unverified]`. The Zinnov 2026 report's framing that "maturity that took a decade is now a design choice made on day one" [C5-1] is marketing, but it does reflect that new mid-market GCCs (35% set up in the last two years [C5-3]) increasingly start with product and data owners rather than graduating to them `[inferred]`.

## 4. Attrition and talent-market constraints, 2024–2026

- **GCC attrition.** EY: 13% (2023) → 11% (2024) → 9% (2025), lowest of any India tech segment; sample averages ~800 headcount per center [C5-4] `[documented]`. Zinnov: 15.4% → 13.3% → 15.1% → 16% (2026), with high-performer attrition 16.5% and **AI/cloud roles at 18–25% with 18–24-month tenures** [C5-7] `[documented]`. The two surveys disagree by 6–7 points; different samples and definitions. Use 9–16% for the site overall and 18–25% for the data/ML roles you actually need.
- **IT services attrition** (LTM, Q2 FY25): Infosys 12.9%, TCS 12.3%, Wipro 14.5% [C5-10] `[documented]`. So GCCs and vendors are now similar on aggregate attrition — the GCC advantage is at the senior/niche end, not the mass.
- **Pay.** GCC average hike 9.8%; AI/ML 21.1%, cybersecurity 20%, NLP 19.2%, top performers 17.2% [C5-7]; GCCs pay 25–40% over IT services at mid-senior levels [C5-11] `[documented, EY via HRKatha]`. Niche-skill hikes ~1.7x standard [C5-8].
- **City.** Zinnov ER&D attrition: Bengaluru 14.3%, Chennai 8.2%, tier-II 10–15% lower [C5-7] `[documented]`. Six cities host 92% of GCCs (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, NCR, Pune, Mumbai, Chennai) [C5-3]. Claims that Hyderabad/Pune run 10–25% cheaper than Bengaluru for data roles appear only in staffing-firm blogs `[folklore-grade]`.
- **Senior scarcity.** 77% of GCC hiring in 2025 was expected to be mid-to-senior, up from 63% [C5-6]; the binding constraint is second-line managers, not engineers [C5-6][C5-9] `[documented, consultant]`. Candidate-to-hire ratios of 7:1–8:1 are normal [C5-9].
- **Supply shock.** ~80,000 roles disappeared at the top-5 IT services firms over 18 months to mid-2025; GCCs added ~200k net in FY26 vs ~110k for IT services [C5-11] `[documented, secondary]`. AI/data hiring is the fastest-inflating segment; EY: 63% of GCCs hiring niche AI/ML, 54% data engineering/BI [C5-4].

## What this evidence says about the charter choice

**1. Pure extension (c) as a *long-term* charter is the documented stagnation path.** The two celebrated graduations took 10–15 years and required hiring product, data science and second-line management in India early; the consultant base rate for graduating in five years is ~27% and the 2007 cohort had a >60% struggle rate. If you choose (c), the evidence says the graduation criteria must be written down at set-up and the PM/DS/manager hires must precede graduation, not follow it. **[judgment]**: a graduation model without a dated ownership commitment is model (b)-without-the-budget.

**2. The distributed-work research rules out "shared ownership of the same work" across a 12.5-hour gap.** Herbsleb-Mockus's 2.5x and Cummings et al.'s time-separation finding both say the interface has to be modular. That favors whole-module ownership — either a domain or a platform — over an extension where India engineers pick tickets off US backlogs. **[judgment]**: for a data org, "pipelines-as-a-service" and core data platform are naturally modular with contract-style interfaces; commerce/fraud data domains are modular only if the PM and the US business counterpart are willing to accept a day's latency on decisions.

**3. Platform ownership (b) is the modal success and the modal drift.** JPMorgan and WBD show it scales; the Citi divestiture and Borowski's "stagnant captive" show it drifts into a cost center when no product outcome depends on it. **[judgment]**: (b) is the safest first charter for a 140-person org converting contractors, *provided* one product-facing domain is also seeded so the site has an outcome the US cannot ship without.

**4. Domain ownership (a) worked where the domain was India-first or self-contained.** No verified case of a global consumer domain handed to a new GCC on day one succeeding. **[judgment]**: pick one domain whose center of gravity can plausibly move (experimentation platform, QoE/sessions telemetry — high-volume, low-regulatory, few US business stakeholders) rather than commerce/fraud, which has the most US stakeholders and the most latency-sensitive decisions.

**5. Talent facts constrain the timeline.** AI/data attrition of 18–25%, second-line-manager scarcity, and vendor control over which contractors convert mean year one goes to stabilizing headcount, not owning domains. **[judgment]**: charter as (d) hybrid — platform ownership now, one domain with a named India PM and a site head with a global title, extension for the rest with a written 24-month graduation test — noting that "hybrid" is what successful sites became, not what they chose.
