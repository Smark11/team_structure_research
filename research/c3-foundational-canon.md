# C3 — The Foundational Canon of Organization Design, Applied to the India-Site Charter Decision

**Agent:** C3 | **Date:** 2026-08-29 | **Status:** Private working memo, not a recommendation

**Scope.** What the academic and practitioner canon of organization design actually says, where it is empirical versus folklore, where the frameworks contradict each other, and what survives all of that as a durable principle for a ~140-person data org deciding the charter of a new India engineering site. ## 1. Galbraith: the Star Model and the information-processing view

Galbraith's Star Model (strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people) is the most-copied org-design framework in existence; McKinsey 7-S and Nadler-Tushman's congruence model are descendants [C3-1]. Its real content is not the five points; it is two claims underneath them.

First, from the 1974 *Interfaces* paper: organization design is an information-processing problem. Task uncertainty determines how much information must be processed during execution; when hierarchy saturates, you have exactly four moves — create slack resources, create self-contained tasks (which *reduce* the need for information processing), or invest in vertical information systems or lateral relations (which *increase* processing capacity) [C3-2] `[documented]`. This is the theoretical spine under everything later called "autonomous teams," "platform," and "data mesh." A domain-owned data site is a self-contained-task strategy. A capability/platform site is a vertical-information-system strategy. A graduating extension model is a lateral-relations strategy that hopes to become self-contained tasks over time.

Second, the alignment claim: the five points must be mutually consistent, and *structure is the least important* of the five to get right first, because processes, rewards, and people can compensate for a mediocre structure but not vice versa [C3-1] `[documented]`. Galbraith is explicit that leaders over-focus on the org chart. For this decision: the reporting line of the India site matters less than whether the planning process, the promotion criteria, and the on-call rewards treat India as a peer.

The reconfigurable/lateral organization work (1997 chapter; revised in *Designing Organizations*; 2010 article) argues that stable structure plus deliberately designed lateral processes (cross-unit teams, integrator roles, matrix) yields reconfigurability without perpetual reorgs [C3-3] `[documented]`. Durable lesson: **design the lateral links as carefully as the boxes.** Most second-site failures are lateral-process failures (planning, code review, escalation), not box failures `[judgment]`.

## 2. Mintzberg: what configuration a data org actually is

Mintzberg's 1980 synthesis proposes five configurations — simple structure, machine bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, divisionalized form, adhocracy — each defined by a dominant coordinating mechanism (direct supervision, standardization of work processes, standardization of skills, standardization of outputs, mutual adjustment) and a dominant "part" of the organization (apex, technostructure, operating core, middle line, support staff) [C3-4] `[documented]`. The 1981 HBR version's thesis is that effectiveness comes from *internal consistency* of the configuration, not from adopting whatever is fashionable [C3-5] `[documented]`.

Which one is a 140-person data org? It is not one; it is two configurations glued together, and this is the source of most of its structural pain `[judgment, inferred from C3-4]`:

- The **core data platform** (ingest, warehouse, orchestration, lineage, quality tooling) behaves like a **machine bureaucracy** trending toward **professional bureaucracy**: work is standardized, outputs are standardized (SLAs, schemas, contracts), and it is coordinated by the technostructure (platform architects, data governance). Mintzberg would predict it centralizes and that its natural coordinating mechanism is standardization of work and outputs.
- The **domain teams** (commerce, experimentation, search, QoE, funnels) are a **professional bureaucracy** on good days (coordination by standardized skills — you hire a data engineer or analytics engineer and trust their training) and an **adhocracy** on bad days (coordination by mutual adjustment, because the question changes every quarter and nobody's playbook covers it).

Mintzberg's warnings apply: professional bureaucracies resist central control; adhocracies are the most expensive configuration to coordinate and drift toward bureaucracy under pressure [C3-4]. His "divisionalized form" — the only configuration coordinated by standardized *outputs* — is the closest analogue to "give India full domain ownership and measure it on outputs," and he is blunt that it works only when divisions are genuinely separable and HQ restrains itself to output control; when HQ intervenes in process, it collapses into a machine bureaucracy with extra layers [C3-4] `[documented]`.

Durable principle: **pick the coordinating mechanism before you pick the box.** If the India site's work will be coordinated by mutual adjustment (adhocracy), distance is fatal, because mutual adjustment is exactly what distance destroys (Section 9). If it can be coordinated by standardized outputs (divisionalized) or standardized skills (professional), distance is survivable.

## 3. Conway's Law, the Inverse Conway Maneuver, and the evidence

Conway's 1968 *Datamation* paper: organizations "are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" [C3-6] `[documented]`. Two things get forgotten. Conway's mechanism is *communication paths*, not reporting lines — a system boundary appears wherever two groups cannot talk cheaply. And his corollary is that because the first design is never the best, "flexibility of organization is important to effective design." He describes a constraint; he does not prescribe a strategy.

The prescriptive flip — the **Inverse Conway Maneuver** — comes from ThoughtWorks (Leroy and Simons, 2010; Tech Radar "Trial" in July 2014): evolve the team structure to promote the architecture you want [C3-10] `[documented]`. Skelton & Pais operationalize it (Section 4).

Evidence base, in descending order of strength:

- **MacCormack, Baldwin & Rusnak (Research Policy, 2012; HBS working paper 2008)** test the "mirroring hypothesis" on matched open-source versus commercial software products with the same function and find that loosely coupled organizations produce significantly more modular products [C3-7] `[documented]`. The natural-experiment design is the strongest test of Conway in the literature. Caveat: it shows *organization → architecture*, in software; nobody has replicated it on data pipelines.
- **Nagappan, Murphy & Basili (ICSE 2008)**, Windows Vista: eight organizational-structure metrics (engineers and ex-engineers touching a binary, depth of master ownership, organizational intersection, etc.) predict failure-proneness with precision/recall around 86%/84%, beating churn, complexity, coverage, and dependencies [C3-8] `[documented]`. The finding that matters here: *diffuse ownership* predicts defects.
- **Bird, Nagappan, Devanbu, Gall & Murphy (ICSE 2009)**, same data: components built by geographically distributed teams showed a *negligible* difference in post-release failures versus collocated ones, smaller still after controlling for developer count [C3-9] `[documented]`. This is the most important empirical result for the India decision and it cuts against the pessimism of Section 9. Microsoft's mitigations were consistent tooling, shared process, long-tenured distributed leads, and site-level ownership. Read with 2008: *distance* did not hurt quality; *diffuse ownership* did.
- **Herbsleb & Grinter (IEEE Software 1999)** — a Lucent case study — find that modular design is "necessary but not sufficient" to make distributed development work, because unanticipated events require communication that the architecture cannot substitute for [C3-11] `[documented]`. Their line "Conway's law and beyond" is the honest summary: the mirror is real, but the mirror does not eliminate coordination.
- **Cataldo, Herbsleb & Carley (ESEM 2008)** formalize "socio-technical congruence": when actual coordination matches the coordination *required* by technical dependencies, modification-request resolution time falls by roughly a third [C3-12] `[documented]`.

Durable reading: Conway is empirically well-supported at the level of "ownership boundaries show up in the artifact" and "diffuse ownership costs quality." It is *not* supported as "draw the org chart and the architecture follows"; the 1999 and 2008 Herbsleb work is explicit that architecture reduces coordination need but never to zero.

## 4. Team Topologies

Skelton & Pais (IT Revolution, 2019; 2nd edition 2025) define four team types — stream-aligned, enabling, complicated-subsystem, platform — and three interaction modes — collaboration, X-as-a-Service, facilitation [C3-13][C3-14] `[documented]`. Their organizing principles are (a) the team is the unit of delivery, (b) team cognitive load is the binding constraint and should be explicitly managed (they borrow Sweller's intrinsic/extraneous/germane taxonomy), (c) Conway's Law should be used deliberately, and (d) platforms exist to reduce stream-aligned teams' extraneous cognitive load, starting from the **thinnest viable platform** — "just enough capability to accelerate delivery," possibly a wiki page curating vendor services [C3-15] `[documented]`.

What they say about geography is thin and mostly in the 2022 *Remote Team Interactions Workbook*: remote work "highlighted existing poor team interactions"; "the physical office was covering up poorly defined teams and poorly defined areas of focus"; the fix is explicit **Team APIs** (what a team owns, how to engage it, working hours, communication channels), deliberate design of inter-team communication, and tracking and removal of team-level dependencies [C3-16] `[documented]`. The book itself favors co-located or same-timezone teams and states the collaboration mode should be time-boxed because it is expensive; I could not fetch the exact page and mark the timezone preference `[inferred]`.

Where Team Topologies is weakest for a data org: it assumes stream-aligned teams can own end-to-end value streams, which in a data org means owning a domain's models, pipelines, metrics, and the product decisions they serve — a stack most data orgs cannot fully staff per domain (Section 8). It also does not address analytics or data-science work whose "flow" is a question, not a feature. Treat it as a vocabulary and a set of constraints, not a blueprint.

## 5. Span of control: what the evidence actually is

The lineage matters because the number "7" is treated as science and is not.

- **Graicunas (1933)** proposed a formula for the *relationships* a manager must attend to (direct, cross, group), which explodes combinatorially — 5 subordinates → 100 relationships, 6 → 222 — and recommended 4–5 at senior levels [C3-17] `[documented, but arithmetic, not evidence]`.
- **Urwick (HBR, 1956)** popularized it ("no supervisor can supervise directly the work of more than five or, at the most, six subordinates whose work interlocks") and disclosed he helped Graicunas write the 1933 paper [C3-18] `[documented]`. The qualifier "whose work interlocks" is the piece everyone drops.
- **Miller (1956)**, "the magical number seven," is about short-term memory chunks; it says nothing about management. Its co-option into span advice is `[folklore]` [C3-19].
- **McKinsey** rejects a single number: player/coach 3–5, coach 6–7, supervisor 8–10, facilitator 11–15, coordinator 15+, driven by work standardization and the manager's own IC load [C3-20] `[consulting synthesis]`. This is Mintzberg's coordinating mechanisms as a headcount table.
- **Google's Project Oxygen** studies manager *behaviors*, not span; it is often cited for span and does not study it [C3-21] `[documented]`. Google's span norms are `[folklore]` in the public record.
- **The 5–8 engineering norm.** Larson: hands-on technical managers "three to five," people-focused managers "five to eight, depending on experience level," and never more than eight [C3-23][C3-24] `[practitioner norm]`.

Bottom line: span is a function of task interlock (Urwick), standardization (McKinsey/Mintzberg), and manager type (Larson), not a constant. `[judgment]`: platform teams doing standardized work tolerate 8–10; domain analytics-engineering teams doing interlocked, uncertain work want 5–7; a remote site argues for the low end, or for a site-local manager rather than a US manager with remote directs.

## 6. Larson: sizing, four states, reorgs

Larson's rules (blog 2018; *An Elegant Puzzle*, Stripe Press 2019) [C3-22][C3-23] `[documented, practitioner]`:

- Teams should be **six to eight** at steady state. To create a team, grow an existing one to eight–ten and bud it into two of four–five. Teams smaller than four are "transitory" or "exploratory." He argues that people who advocate smaller teams are "cavalier in ignoring the downsides."
- Every team is in one of **four states** — falling behind, treading water, repaying debt, innovating — and the fix is state-specific: add people when falling behind, reduce WIP when treading water, add time when repaying debt, and *keep slack* when innovating rather than reassigning it. Crucially, **shift resources to the most-behind team; do not spread thin** [C3-25].
- **Reorg tests** [C3-24]: "Is the problem structural?" (does it require changing communication, decision friction, or attention?); "Are you reorganizing to work around a broken relationship?" (the worst reason — "karma always comes due"); "Does the problem already exist?" (do not pre-solve); "Are the conditions temporary?" If a reorg passes, his sequence is: validate necessity → project one-year headcount → set manager:engineer ratio → identify logical teams and groups → staff → get leadership commitment → roll out with reasoning, per-person impact documentation, and availability for the fallout.
- **Organizational debt**: Larson's usage is narrower than the term's originator (Steve Blank, 2015, who meant all the people/culture compromises made to ship) [C3-27]. Larson treats organizational debt as structural problems that never become the most pressing thing, and singles out "organizational risk" as its volatile subset [C3-26] `[documented]`.

The tension with Section 5: Larson's *team-size floor* of six (steady state) and his *span ceiling* of eight are the same number seen from two sides; a site with 12 people cannot honor both floor and ceiling and also have two domains. This is the arithmetic that decides whether a remote site can be "full domain owner" of anything: if you cannot put 6–8 people on it, Larson says you do not have a team, you have a project.

## 7. Accelerate and DORA — and the limits for data

*Accelerate* (2018) reports survey research across the State of DevOps program: four delivery metrics (lead time, deployment frequency, MTTR, change-fail rate) predict organizational performance, and among the capabilities that predict the metrics is **loosely coupled architecture** — teams can make large-scale design changes without permission, complete work without fine-grained coordination with other teams, deploy and release independently, and test without an integrated environment [C3-28][C3-29] `[documented, survey-based]`. Their memorable claim is that *architecture* mattered more than *technology*: high performance was possible on mainframes if teams and systems were loosely coupled. DORA's 2023 report adds that user-centric teams, faster code review, and generative culture correlate with performance, and treats platform engineering as a first-class topic [C3-30] `[documented, survey-based]`.

Limits for data orgs `[judgment, inferred]`: the four metrics assume the unit of change is a service deploy, whereas a model change, a metric redefinition, or a backfill has radically different blast radius and reversibility; "deploy independently" is aspirational when every domain reads every other domain's tables — the coupling is in the data, not the code; and nobody has validated the metrics on work whose output is a decision. The transferable part is the *structural* finding — teams needing little cross-team coordination outperform — which is Galbraith 1974 and Thompson 1967 with a survey behind it. For India the question is not "can India deploy" but "how many US teams must India talk to per change."

## 8. The data-org literature

This literature is almost entirely practitioner, almost entirely unreplicated, and it recycles the same three-way choice: **centralized**, **embedded/decentralized**, **hybrid (hub-and-spoke)**.

- **DalleMule & Davenport (HBR 2017)** is the closest thing to a theory: data strategy runs on an offense–defense spectrum (defense = quality, security, compliance, single source of truth; offense = revenue, analytics, speed), defense wants centralized control and offense wants flexibility, and the balance dictates structure [C3-42] `[documented]`. Commerce/fraud data in a media company is defense-heavy; experimentation and browse are offense-heavy. This predicts they should not be organized identically.
- **Locally Optimistic**: Kaminsky's "One Size Fits None" (2018) says structure depends on ambient technical ability, distribution of analytical skill, and engineering support, not a template [C3-36]; "The Data Business Partnership" says embedded practitioners should still report to the data org, HR-business-partner style, with a strong central team owning models and metrics [C3-37] `[practitioner]`.
- **dbt Labs**: Vaughan (2024) reframes the question as "what, exactly, should be centralized, and what decentralized?" and reports a domain-based hybrid that "works for 10 people…could probably work for 20. Beyond that, we don't know" [C3-38]; Handy makes the same move — central platform, "data expertise everywhere" [C3-39] `[practitioner]`. The analytics-engineering reorg wave (2018–2022) was, in Mintzberg's terms, a shift from mutual adjustment to standardization of skills.
- **Mode**: the "pulmonary model" — rotate analysts between central and embedded seats to build domain context without losing methodological consistency [C3-40] `[practitioner]`.
- **Stancil, "Disband the analytics team" (2024)**: analytics has over-promised; either accept it as operational support or embed the people in product/engineering/business units and stop maintaining a department [C3-35] `[opinion]`. He has separately argued data mesh is "delegated," not decentralized, because a central authority still sets standards — Mintzberg's divisionalized-form caveat restated.
- **Sanderson** on data contracts: ownership attaches to the *producer* once an asset is shown to have value; contracts define the failure state and must be enforced in CI/CD or they are decorative [C3-41] `[practitioner]`. This is Nagappan 2008 turned into a mechanism.
- **Data mesh as org design.** Dehghani's four principles — domain ownership, data as a product, self-serve platform, federated computational governance [C3-31][C3-32] `[documented]` — are a Team Topologies diagram applied to analytical data. The critiques are consistent: the binding constraint is people, not technology; domains lack data-competent staff and will not fund them; "data domains" get created as lip service by rebranding IT teams without decision rights; upskilling is under-budgeted; platforms get over-built before value is shown; governance stalls on stakeholder alignment (Thoughtworks' own 2026 retrospective) [C3-33] `[vendor retrospective]`. Kwan (2025) argues the reverse — mesh fails because the *infrastructure* (versioning, contracts, consistency) was never built for decentralization, so reorgs relocate bottlenecks [C3-34] `[opinion]`. Both are probably true: mesh needs staffing *and* tooling most 140-person orgs lack.
- **Data platform as a platform team.** Netflix is the canonical strong-central-platform / distributed-analytics-engineering example [C3-53] `[inferred — post body not fetched]`: hub-and-spoke at ~30x this org's scale.

What the data literature lacks: any controlled comparison, any replication, and any treatment of geography. Domain-versus-capability at a remote site is unaddressed.

## 9. Distance: the empirical record on second sites

This is where the canon is strongest and most ignored.

- **Herbsleb & Mockus (IEEE TSE 2003)**, Lucent change data plus surveys: cross-site work items took about **2.5x as long** as comparable single-site items — not because individuals were slower but because more people were involved, informal communication was scarcer, and finding the right person took longer [C3-43] `[documented]`. This is the real basis of "Herbsleb's law"; a 2025 retrospective in the same journal confirms it has held.
- **Olson & Olson, "Distance Matters" (HCI 2000)**: remote collaboration works only with high **common ground**, loosely coupled work, **collaboration readiness**, and **technology readiness** — and technology does not substitute for the first two [C3-44] `[documented]`.
- **Carmel & Agarwal (IEEE Software 2001)**: distance is geographic, temporal, and cultural; the tactics that work are to **reduce intensive collaboration** across sites (each site owns separable work), reduce cultural distance (liaisons, bridgeheads), and reduce temporal distance (overlap windows, asynchronous methods) [C3-45] `[documented]`. This is the direct case for site-level ownership of separable work.
- **Carmel, Espinosa & Dubinsky (JMIS 2010)** on follow-the-sun: duration falls only when handoff efficiency is high and reduced within-site coordination outweighs the added cross-site coordination — rarely, for design-heavy work [C3-46] `[documented]`. Follow-the-sun for data engineering is `[judgment]` a trap unless the work is operational (on-call, backfills, incidents).
- **Hinds & Bailey (Org Science 2003)**: distributed teams have more task, affective, and process conflict, driven by reduced shared context, unequal information, and technology mediation; the countermeasures are shared identity and context, not tools [C3-47] `[documented]`.
- **Bird et al. 2009** (Section 3) is the counterweight [C3-9]. The reconciliation is Olson & Olson's: Microsoft had high common ground (one process, one toolchain, long-tenured leads, site-level ownership) and reduced coupling. Distance hurts *coupled, low-common-ground* work and is survivable for *decoupled, high-common-ground* work.

## Where the frameworks disagree

**Team Topologies' stream-aligned bias vs. data-mesh reality.** Skelton & Pais say most teams should be stream-aligned and platform should be "thinnest viable"; Dehghani says the same for data. The 2026 Thoughtworks retrospective and the practitioner literature say most orgs cannot staff data-competent domain teams and end up with a thick central platform doing the domain work anyway [C3-33][C3-38]. The disagreement is not in principle but in staffing arithmetic: Larson's six-person floor times eight domains is 48 people of domain data engineering before you build a platform. At 140 total that is arithmetically possible and financially unlikely.

**Conway's Law vs. deliberately mirrored design.** Conway says the org constrains the architecture; the Inverse Conway Maneuver says design the org to get the architecture. Herbsleb & Grinter and Cataldo show the mirror is necessary but never sufficient — coordination need persists beyond any architecture [C3-11][C3-12]. So "give India a clean module and the coordination problem goes away" is half true: it goes down 30% (Cataldo), not to zero. The other disagreement: data mesh mirrors the org onto the *data*, but warehouse data is reciprocally interdependent (Thompson) in a way service code is not — every domain reads every other domain's facts. Conway on data produces silos faster than it produces modularity.

**Mintzberg's adhocracy vs. Galbraith's process emphasis.** Mintzberg says innovative work is coordinated by mutual adjustment and resists formalization; Galbraith says design the lateral processes explicitly. For a remote site these are opposed: mutual adjustment is what distance destroys (Olson & Olson), so a site doing adhocratic work needs Galbraith's formalized lateral processes — which Mintzberg predicts will slow the work. There is no clean resolution; the practical answer is to route adhocratic work to whichever site has the stakeholders, and formalize only the interfaces `[judgment]`.

**Span-of-control norms vs. Larson's team-size floor.** Spans of 5–8 and teams of 6–8 are consistent only if every manager has exactly one team. The moment a site has 12 people and two domains, you either violate the floor (two teams of six, fine) or the ceiling (one manager, twelve directs, not fine) or add a layer (a site lead over two managers, which at 12 people is a 1:4 manager ratio that no CFO likes). The frameworks are silent on this; it is decided by budget.

**Case studies vs. frameworks.** Spotify's 2012 whitepaper was a snapshot of ~30 squads that Kniberg said was not a framework [C3-48]; Lee (2020) reports it was aspirational, never fully implemented, and dismantled in favor of conventional management as the company tripled — autonomy without alignment, matrix reporting with no accountable owner [C3-49] `[documented]`. Amazon's single-threaded leader is the opposite: one accountable person, a separable team, and the explicit lesson that separability of the *work* is the precondition [C3-50] `[documented]`. Netflix runs a strong central platform under distributed analytics engineering [C3-53] — the hub-and-spoke that the mesh literature both recommends and complains is not decentralized enough. Through-line: what survived had a single accountable owner per separable unit of work; what failed had autonomy without ownership.

## Durable principles

1. **Task interdependence dictates the coordination mechanism, and the mechanism dictates what distance can tolerate.** Pooled → standardize; sequential → plan; reciprocal → mutual adjustment, which requires co-location or heroic effort [C3-52][C3-4][C3-44] `[documented]`.
2. **Self-contained tasks are the cheapest way to reduce information-processing load; lateral processes are the most expensive way to increase capacity.** Prefer the former across sites [C3-2][C3-45].
3. **Ownership clarity predicts quality; diffuse ownership predicts defects.** This is the best-replicated Conway-adjacent result and it holds regardless of geography [C3-8][C3-9].
4. **Distance per se does not degrade quality; coupling and low common ground do.** Cross-site coupled work is ~2.5x slower, but decoupled work with shared process and tooling is not measurably worse [C3-43][C3-9][C3-44].
5. **Architecture reduces coordination need but never eliminates it; budget for the residual.** Congruence between coordination need and actual coordination is worth ~30% on resolution time [C3-11][C3-12].
6. **Structure is the least important Star point to get right first; processes, rewards, and people compensate for structure but not the reverse.** [C3-1]
7. **Internal consistency of the configuration beats fashion.** Mixing divisional output-control with machine-bureaucracy process-control is the most common failure [C3-5][C3-4].
8. **Span of control is a function of task interlock and standardization, not a constant; "7" is folklore descended from a 1933 arithmetic exercise and a 1956 memory experiment.** Ranges: 3–5 for player-coaches, 6–8 for coaching managers, 8–15 for standardized work [C3-17][C3-18][C3-19][C3-20][C3-23].
9. **A team is six to eight people; below four it is a project.** Concentrate headcount on the most-behind team rather than spreading thin [C3-23][C3-25].
10. **Reorg only for structural problems that already exist and are not temporary; never to route around a person.** [C3-24]
11. **Platforms should start thin and be treated as products with internal customers; they exist to reduce the cognitive load of the teams they serve, not to accumulate the work domains will not fund.** [C3-15][C3-13][C3-33]
12. **Loosely coupled teams (able to change, test, and deploy without cross-team permission) outperform, independent of technology stack.** Transfer the structural finding, not the four metrics, to data work [C3-28][C3-29].
13. **Producers own data quality; contracts define the failure state and must be enforced mechanically.** [C3-41][C3-31]
14. **A single accountable owner per separable unit of work is what survived in every case study; autonomy without accountable ownership is what failed.** [C3-50][C3-49][C3-48]
15. **Remote work exposes poorly defined teams; the fix is explicit Team APIs, tracked dependencies, and deliberately designed inter-team communication, not tooling.** [C3-16][C3-47][C3-45]

## Implications for the decision

**Domain vs. capability ownership at a remote site.** The canon converges on one test: *how reciprocally interdependent is the work with people at the other site?* Carmel & Agarwal, Olson & Olson, and Herbsleb & Mockus all say the same thing from different angles — give the distant site work that is separable and coordinated by standardized outputs or skills, not by mutual adjustment [C3-45][C3-44][C3-43]. That argues for either (a) full ownership of a *whole* domain including its stakeholders' primary contact, or (b) ownership of a platform capability with a clean interface. It argues *against* the common middle: India owning "the pipelines" for a domain whose analysts, PMs, and stakeholders are in the US, because that is Thompson's reciprocal interdependence stretched across 10.5 hours, and Herbsleb's 2.5x applies to every ticket. **[judgment]** For this org, the domains where stakeholders are least US-bound and outputs most standardizable (fraud, commerce data quality, subscriber-state modeling — DalleMule's "defense" work) are the plausible full-ownership candidates; experimentation, browse, and funnel work are adhocratic and stakeholder-adjacent, and belong where the stakeholders sit.

**Capability/platform ownership** has the stronger evidentiary case for a new site: platform work is Mintzberg's machine/professional bureaucracy, coordinated by standardization; it is what Team Topologies and Bird et al. describe as the decoupled, high-common-ground work distance tolerates [C3-4][C3-9][C3-15]. The risks the canon names: the platform becomes the dumping ground for unfunded domain work (Thoughtworks 2026 retrospective) [C3-33], and a platform team with no internal customers on its own site loses the "platform as product" feedback loop. **[judgment]** Mitigate by giving India at least one domain *consumer* of its own platform work.

**The graduating extension model.** The canon is uneasy about it. Its virtue is Galbraith's — it starts with lateral relations and moves toward self-contained tasks as common ground accrues, which is what Olson & Olson say has to happen first [C3-2][C3-44]. Its vice is that during the "extension" phase, ownership is by definition diffuse (US owns, India contributes), which is exactly the Nagappan 2008 predictor of defects, and the Lee/Spotify pattern of responsibility without accountability [C3-8][C3-49]. **[judgment]** The model works only with (1) a written graduation criterion per team (Team API + ownership transfer date), (2) a site lead in India who is the single-threaded owner of the graduation itself, and (3) Larson's floor honored — do not "graduate" anything smaller than six people. Otherwise the extension phase becomes permanent, because nothing in the incentive structure (Star Model: rewards) pushes a US owner to hand off.

**What it implies for the whole org.** Whatever India owns, the US org must be re-cut to *not* own it. Half-ownership is the one configuration every source in this memo says fails.
